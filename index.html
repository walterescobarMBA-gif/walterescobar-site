Why housing

Los Angeles can acknowledge progress and still demand much more.

Homelessness is not an abstraction here. It is visible in neighborhoods, parks, transit stations, sidewalks, vehicles, shelters, and the daily lives of Angelenos.

LAHSA’s 2026 count estimated 45,194 people experiencing homelessness in the City of Los Angeles, including 29,115 people experiencing unsheltered homelessness. LAHSA described the 2026 results as a stall after two years of regional declines; the year-over-year changes were not considered statistically significant.

That nuance matters. Progress should be recognized. But the scale of homelessness should also keep the public conversation centered on outcomes: helping people come inside, move into permanent housing, and remain housed.

2026 snapshot source: Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority, July 24, 2026 .

What I believe

A housing response should be judged by what happens to people.

01

People before process

Administrative systems should support housing stability, not become barriers that people must survive.

02

Outcomes over activity

Meetings, referrals, contracts, and expenditures are inputs. A safe and stable home is the outcome.

03

Public money, public accountability

Publicly funded housing work should be transparent enough to show what was promised, what was delivered, and what changed.

04

Dignity without qualification

A person’s appearance, background, disability, identity, income, or circumstances should not diminish the urgency of helping them reach safety.

05

Clarity is a service

People should be able to understand who is responsible, what happens next, what records exist, and how to ask for help.

06

Stability is the goal

Getting indoors is important. The deeper objective is durable housing stability and a realistic path forward.

Independent initiative

Housing Accountability Archive

Housing Accountability Archive is an independent, participant-founded public-interest housing research and accountability initiative. It organizes records, tests what can be independently verified, compares documented practice with governing standards, and publishes source-based accountability work.

The work is designed to make complicated housing administration more understandable to participants, researchers, reporters, agencies, providers, and oversight bodies while preserving clear evidence boundaries.

Companion framework

Administrative Displacement

Administrative Displacement is a public-interest educational framework for examining how administrative processes—deadlines, notices, handoffs, records, review pathways, and format requirements—can create or intensify housing instability.

It translates documented patterns into plain language so participants, advocates, researchers, reporters, and oversight bodies can recognize the mechanisms, document what happened, and ask better questions without treating the framework itself as a legal conclusion.

About Walter

I came to this work by navigating housing systems firsthand.

What began as an effort to understand my own path through housing systems became a broader interest in how those systems work for the people who depend on them.

I became interested in the spaces between policy and practice: whether a participant can understand the next step, whether responsibility is clear when multiple organizations are involved, whether records can reconstruct what happened, and whether administrative activity ultimately advances housing stability.

I am especially interested in Los Angeles because this is home. I want to see a city where urgency is matched by competence, compassion is matched by follow-through, and public investment can be traced to meaningful housing outcomes.

That experience also changed my long-term direction. I am pursuing a legal career because I want the training to understand rights, procedure, evidence, and institutional responsibility at a deeper level—and to put that knowledge to work in public-interest service. Housing is where that direction became concrete; it is not where my curiosity ends.
